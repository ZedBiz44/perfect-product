## Six-pack principle
Jack's words, summarized: the perfect product is like a six-pack, not a \$40 cocktail or a stop at the bar.
- It's cheap.
- It's easy to grab.
- It's satisfying right away.
- It keeps paying off through the week.
Each week it delivers **ONE key usable thing**, not buried in volume. Jack's examples of what not to do: Jeff Hunter's hour-long OpenClaw video, and later Dan Kennedy newsletters.
An optional Q&A can come after. The weekly drop could be an email, a video, a module, or a kit.

## Three new angles (Sep 28, evening)
Ideas and questions, not tested plans.
### 1. Run it like a newspaper syndicate
Syndicates sold the same columns and comics to hundreds of newspapers. They didn't write everything; they found good voices, packaged them and did the selling. Apply that to communities: recruit retired pros (contractors, bankers, ad people) who each know a "finish the edges" trick for a niche. AI turns their knowledge into the weekly drop. Jack owns the catalog and the buyer list. The business stops depending on Jack knowing everything, and a contributor catalog plus paying subscribers is a sellable asset.
### 2. Let tool companies pay, so owners get it free
The biggest Facebook AI/OpenClaw groups have huge audiences but no product and likely won't pay \$27. The only sellers in those feeds are tool companies (AgentMail, setup funnels, hosting). Flip who pays: the drop is free to owners, and each week one tool company pays to be the featured example tool in the concept. The weekly "comment the word" post doubles as a lead generator for the sponsor. This is the trade-magazine model: free to readers, paid by advertisers. **Open question:** do these tool companies already pay group admins or influencers? Easy to research.
### 3. Sell to groups that already have a member-content budget
Skool owners spend little. Some groups have bought member content for decades: chambers of commerce, business improvement associations, franchise head offices, farm co-ops and commodity associations. They must show members value every month. Jack's 25 years of local marketing makes him a natural supplier to chambers. His farm network opens ag associations, where he's the "how to get found" guy for farm-store and direct-sale members, not the ag expert. This brings the farm idea off the back burner without asking Jack to be something he's not. **Open question:** do chambers and co-ops buy this as a subscription, or only one-off?
### Strongest combination
1 plus 3: a syndicate selling to groups with budgets, with Skool owners as a side market.
## Three agency angles (Sep 28, night)
Ideas and questions, not tested plans. Built on Z3-Thoughts (who loses money when the end user's problem isn't solved; ingredient company), Mary's Thoughts (build the shelf), NotionAI-Thoughts (performance data as the moat), and the Shovel page. This is NOT the rejected GHL snapshot kit: GHL is only the delivery pipe, and the buyer pays for value to their clients.
### 1. The client-retention drop, priced per client
Agencies lose money every time a client quits, and clients quit when they stop seeing what the agency does for them. Supply one short, useful "finish the edges" idea for local business owners each week, branded as the agency's, sent to every client automatically. The agency looks proactive every week without lifting a finger. Price per client seat (a small monthly fee per client), so a 1,000-client agency pays far more than a 50-client one without any extra work from us. This is the Shovel concept pointed at the one place Jack's 25 years of local marketing is strongest.
**Open question:** what do big agencies send clients each week now, and would they pay per client for it?
### 2. Give agencies something new to sell to clients they already have
An agency's easiest growth is selling more to existing clients. Build a shelf of ready campaigns tied to local-business moments (slow January, first snowfall, back to school, Mother's Day, tax season), in niche editions (HVAC, dental, restaurants, trades). The agency pushes a campaign into every relevant client account and charges its clients for it. We sell the license; the agency keeps the markup. They pay us gladly because every campaign makes them money.
**Open question:** do agencies currently sell seasonal add-ons, and what do they charge clients for them?
### 3. The benchmark nobody else can see
If the same campaigns run for thousands of businesses across many agencies, we can see (with agency opt-in) what actually worked by industry and season. Package that as a monthly "what worked for local businesses this month" report the agency shows clients in reviews. It proves value, keeps clients, and the data grows harder to copy every month. That data set is the sellable asset.
**Open question:** will agencies share campaign results, and what privacy rules apply?
### Spin-out beyond GHL
The buyer is anyone with a book of small-business clients who loses money when those clients leave: all marketing agencies, bookkeepers and accountants, insurance brokers, payment and POS companies, web hosts, business banks. Jack's banking background makes lenders and accountants an especially natural fit.
### Strongest combination
All three stack: #1 gets in the door and keeps clients, #2 makes the agency money, #3 is the moat and the exit value.
## Jack's verdict on the agency angles (Sep 28, night)
- **The cold beer test:** a buyer wants it so badly they crack it open before they get home. None of the ideas below pass it yet. Keep them in the hopper to mix with other ideas.
- **Benchmark report (renamed "Think and Grow Rich"):** like Napoleon Hill's book, it pulls together what actually works across many businesses. Jack thinks it's a good idea and a strong stickiness piece, but not the main business.
- **Weekly retention drop and seasonal campaign shelf:** basically the same idea (pulling something out of a magic hat for agencies to hand to clients). Good add-ons, not a business.
## Cold beer swings (Sep 28, night)
1. **Save Kit.** A rescue pack to use when a client says they want to cancel: a one-page results letter, a quick-win campaign, and a script for the save call. **Jack's verdict:** if you need a save kit, you're already dead. It's in the hopper only.
2. **Cash This Week.** A niche-written campaign that reactivates a client's old customer list and books jobs within 7 days. **Jack's verdict:** that's the basic client reactivation plan every GHL owner already starts with. It's in the hopper only.
3. **The Door Opener (third swing).** Every week, one tested, niche-specific "free win" an agency can set up for a *prospect* (not a client), such as a Google profile fix that gets dentists more calls, backed by Think and Grow Rich data. The agency's pitch is "I'll set this up free this week, and if it works, let's talk." It's aimed at the agency's biggest pain, which is getting new clients, not keeping old ones. **Open question:** is winning new clients really the pain agency owners would crack open on the drive home, and how is this different from the free audits they already use?
