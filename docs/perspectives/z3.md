## Overview
The Perfect Product work is converging on something broader than finding one clever offer. The strongest opportunity may be to combine three layers: a repeatable product-manufacturing capability, a recurring product that removes a real weekly obligation for the buyer, and distribution through people who already own the audience.
The key shift is to stop treating AI, OpenClaw, GoHighLevel, agents, research, templates, and content as things Jack must sell directly. They can become the manufacturing infrastructure behind the product. The customer buys the finished result, not the machinery.
This direction also fits the four Perfect Product bars: repeat/revenue potential, build-once leverage, creation of a sellable asset, and reduced dependence on Jack's daily presence. It also addresses the recurring problems found throughout the research: Jack does not want to be a community host or influencer, local businesses often require too much education and onboarding, and building a new audience from scratch makes otherwise-good products economically weak.
## The three strategic thoughts
### The product may actually be a Weekly Operating System, not content
The Shovel Your Biz-Shit concept is closer to the opportunity than it first appears, but the product should not be framed as content for community owners.
The real promise is:
> **You never have to figure out what useful thing you're giving your customers or members this week.**
That is an operational dependency rather than a content subscription.
Instead of selling a concept, several posts, a checklist, prompt, or graphic as separate pieces, the product can become a completed weekly campaign-in-a-box. The buyer opens it and thinks: **Good. This week is handled.**
A weekly kit could contain the teaching piece, engagement piece, downloadable asset, follow-up, email, graphic, and short video script. The exact components matter less than eliminating the recurring job.
This also suggests the market should not be restricted to Skool or community owners. The same underlying product could potentially serve:
- Membership owners
- Coaches
- Associations
- Franchises
- SaaS companies educating customers
- Agencies serving a vertical
- Newsletters and publishers
- Chambers
- Vendors with dealer networks
A useful category description may be **Recurring Customer-Value Kits**.
The strategic distinction is important: we are not helping customers create more content. We are taking ownership of one recurring obligation in their business. That should improve retention because cancelling means the weekly job lands back on their desk.
### Stop looking only for Jack's perfect niche and find the perfect distributor
Much of the research asks: **Who should Jack sell to?**
A potentially more valuable question is:
> **Who already owns 1,000–100,000 of the people who should use this product?**
The Carex Hip Kit is an important clue. Its strength is not merely the bundle. It enters an existing medical distribution system at the moment the buyer needs it.
Distribution therefore may deserve to become a fifth bar of the Perfect Product definition rather than merely one desirable trait.
A strong inexpensive product with no distribution can remain a hobby. A fairly ordinary inexpensive product embedded in somebody else's established distribution can become a substantial business.
Instead of designing only for:
**Jack → thousands of individual customers**
we should deliberately investigate:
**Jack → a small number of distributors → hundreds or thousands of end customers**
Potential distributors could include Skool consultants, associations, software companies, niche publishers, agencies, industry suppliers, directories, franchise systems, and creators with large audiences but weak recurring offers.
White-label or licensed versions deserve serious consideration. The distributor keeps the audience, relationship, and brand. ZedBiz becomes the supplier behind the scenes.
That model fits Jack particularly well because he does not need to become the influencer, niche celebrity, or community personality. **Jack/ZedBiz can be the factory.**
### We may be building a Product Factory rather than searching for one Perfect Product
The research continues to ask what specific thing should be produced each week. A different approach is to build the machine capable of repeatedly discovering and producing narrowly targeted products.
ZedBiz already has much of the raw infrastructure:
- Multiple AI models and agents
- Research capability
- Content and marketing capability
- Automation
- GoHighLevel and WordPress
- Canva and creative production
- Templates, prompts, checklists, workflows, and graphics
- Decades of practical business and marketing experience
The more valuable long-term asset may therefore be a **ZedBiz Product Engine**.
Its basic pipeline could be:
**Market → current problem → research → useful concept → practical asset → campaign → QA → packaged product → market test**
That engine could manufacture many kinds of products:
- Weekly community/customer kits
- Association member kits
- Seasonal local-business kits
- OpenClaw operator packs
- Marketing kits
- WordPress packs
- Rural-business packs
- Promotion kits
- Decision kits
Most experiments do not need to become permanent businesses. The advantage of the engine is that product hypotheses can be produced and tested inexpensively.
This changes the strategic objective from:
> **Discover the Perfect Product before committing.**
to:
> **Build a system that can repeatedly discover Perfect Products through real market evidence.**
When one product starts selling disproportionately well, that is when we narrow the focus, automate production, add recurring revenue, recruit distribution partners, deepen the moat, and turn it into a sellable standalone asset.
## Combined direction
The strongest combined thesis coming out of this review is:
> **Product Factory → Recurring Operating Kit → Distributed through people who already own the audience.**
This structure addresses several problems that have repeatedly killed earlier ideas:
- Jack does not need to become a guru.
- Jack does not need to host another community.
- ZedBiz does not need to build a huge audience before making sales.
- AI infrastructure gains a direct economic purpose without AI itself needing to be the offer.
- New product ideas can be tested rather than debated indefinitely.
- Successful products can eventually become independent sellable assets.
The most important conceptual shift is that the agents, AI models, automation, GHL, research systems, and creative tools are potentially **the manufacturing floor — not the product being sold.**
That may be the clearest role yet for the infrastructure ZedBiz has been building.
## Trial Phase and MVP
The next move should **not** be to build the full product. First, run a short smoke-test phase to discover which problem causes real buyer action.
Social engagement is useful as an early signal, but likes and agreeable comments are weak evidence. The evidence ladder should move from engagement → sample request → email/signup → payment → actual use.
### 10–14 day smoke test
Test three different angles that all lead toward the same underlying Product Factory.
#### Angle A — The Weekly Headache
Target community, membership, coaching, and customer-education businesses.
Test whether the recurring obligation itself hurts:
> You run a paid community or membership. Monday comes around again. Now you need something genuinely useful to give members this week — not another AI-generated “5 Tips” post. What takes longer every week: deciding **what** to give them, creating it, or getting members to actually use it?
The goal is to learn whether this is a sufficiently annoying and recurring job.
#### Angle B — The Quiet Supplier
Test the distributor/white-label thesis:
> Instead of selling another marketing tool, what if somebody quietly supplied the finished campaigns and assets your business needs every week? You put your name on it. Your customers get the value. You don't have to create the damn thing. Useful — or solving a problem nobody really has?
This angle can be floated to consultants, agencies, associations, creators, SaaS businesses, and other organizations that already own an audience.
#### Angle C — “This Week Is Handled”
Test the closest version of the actual offer:
> What would it be worth to open one email every Monday morning and think: **Good. This week is handled.** Inside is one useful thing for your customers or members, the posts to introduce it, the email, follow-up, graphic, and everything needed to use it. Not 100 templates. Not a course. One finished weekly campaign.
Use a real call to action such as:
> **Want a sample week? Comment WEEK or send me a DM.**
The key metric is not likes. Track qualified DMs, sample requests, email signups, and eventually purchases.
### Build the MVP backwards from the winning signal
Do not build software, a portal, 52 weeks of material, or a fully automated Product Factory.
The first MVP can be **three finished weekly kits**, created mostly manually.
Choose one audience based on the smoke-test response and build three consecutive editions.
A sample weekly kit could contain:
- One genuinely useful concept
- One polished downloadable asset
- Main teaching/post
- Engagement post or poll
- Follow-up post
- Customer/member email
- Graphic
- 60-second video script
- Simple owner instructions
- White-label version
The goal is not to demonstrate every future feature. The goal is to make the core promise real enough that someone can use it and decide whether it is worth paying for repeatedly.
### Ask for money quickly
After someone receives the sample, offer a simple founding pilot.
Example:
> **Founding Pilot**
> One complete ready-to-use customer/member campaign every week for four weeks.
> **\$29–\$49 for the first month.**
> Limited founding group.
A handful of paying customers is more valuable evidence than a large amount of social engagement.
An illustrative early signal worth continuing might be:
**10+ qualified sample requests → 5+ serious signups/conversations → 3+ paying buyers**
These are learning thresholds, not permanent KPIs. If nobody pays, change the problem, promise, audience, or packaging before investing in more infrastructure.
### Build the Product Factory while fulfilling the MVP
The MVP has a second purpose: begin constructing the internal Product Factory through actual production rather than designing it theoretically.
Use a simple internal pipeline:
**Opportunity → Research → Concept → Asset → Campaign → QA → Package → Deliver**
Run each MVP edition through the existing AI and agent infrastructure and record:
- Human time required
- AI/agent time
- Production cost
- Where quality breaks
- What still requires Jack
- What can be templated
- What can eventually be automated
- What parts are reusable across markets
One particularly important experiment is to take **one core concept** and manufacture several vertical editions, for example:
**Community Coach → Local Business Consultant → SaaS Customer Success**
If roughly 70–80% of the production process can remain common while the final layer creates meaningful niche specificity, the Product Factory starts to have attractive economics.
If every niche requires rebuilding from scratch, that is equally valuable evidence and should change the strategy.
### Suggested 14-day experiment
**Days 1–4:** Publish 6–9 problem/offer posts testing several angles.
**Days 5–6:** Create one simple landing page offering a Sample Week.
**Days 7–9:** Produce one excellent sample kit and deliver it to people who raised their hands.
**Days 10–11:** Offer the four-week founding pilot and ask for payment.
**Days 12–14:** Produce the first paid edition manually through the embryonic Product Factory.
At the end, evaluate behavior rather than opinions:
**Which buyer responded? → Which promise pulled? → Who paid? → What did they actually use? → Can we manufacture it efficiently? → Can somebody else distribute it?**
### Governing principle
Do not improve or automate the factory until there is evidence people want what comes out of it.
The objective of the first trial is therefore twofold:
**Prove demand for the output while learning how to manufacture the output repeatedly.**
That avoids building an impressive machine before proving there is a market for what it produces.
## Second-Layer Insights
These ideas come from stepping back from individual product concepts and combining patterns across the Perfect Product research. They are not recommendations to launch or test a specific business yet. Their purpose is to widen the search space and expose business structures we had not previously put together.
### 1. The Perfect Product may be a Product Ingredient Company
We have repeatedly assumed that ZedBiz needs to own the finished product sold to the end user. That may be the wrong level of the value chain.
Combine the [PLR.me](http://PLR.me) model, the Carex Hip Kit pattern, white-label distribution, channel owners, and the Product Factory concept and a different business appears:
> **ZedBiz manufactures the valuable ingredient that makes somebody else's existing product better.**
The product does not necessarily need to be weekly content. It could be a reusable implementation, engagement, customer-success, decision, or marketing layer that another company bolts onto what it already sells.
Examples of the structure:
- A course company adds a **90-Day Implementation Layer** to its course.
- An association includes a **Member Business Toolkit** with membership.
- A SaaS company gives customers a **Customer Success Pack** that helps them get a result from the software.
- A franchise supplies every franchisee with a **Local Marketing Pack**.
- A directory gives premium advertisers a **Vendor Growth Kit**.
- A consultant includes a **Client Action System** with every engagement.
This changes the sales proposition. We are not asking the buyer to purchase another standalone product. We help them make their **existing product more valuable, useful, sticky, differentiated, or sellable**.
Licensing and white-label economics also become natural because the same underlying intellectual property can be supplied repeatedly to multiple channel owners.
#### Question worth pursuing
> **Which businesses already have customers but have a weak “what happens after they buy?” layer?**
That may be a more interesting hunting ground than asking what consumers need.
### 2. The opportunity may actually be B2B2C — find who loses money when the end user's problem remains unsolved
Across several ideas we keep finding the same structure:
- The **end user experiences the problem**.
- The end user may be a poor customer for ZedBiz.
- A business upstream financially benefits when that end user's problem is solved.
Examples:
**Rural property buyer has uncertainty → realtor or lender benefits from a better-qualified buyer.**
**Community member becomes inactive → community owner loses retention and recurring revenue.**
**Course student gets stuck → course creator suffers poor completion, testimonials, referrals, and repeat sales.**
**Software user fails to implement → SaaS company faces churn.**
**Franchisee struggles with marketing → franchisor suffers weaker locations and brand performance.**
**Association member gets little practical value → association faces weaker renewal.**
**Advertiser does not know how to market → directory or publisher risks advertiser churn.**
This is more than finding a distributor.
The distributor can become **the paying customer** because solving the end user's problem improves the distributor's own economics.
That suggests a new Perfect Product question:
> **Who loses money when the end user's problem isn't solved?**
Instead of only searching for people experiencing pain, look one level upstream for organizations with a financial incentive to solve that pain repeatedly and at scale.
This may reveal markets where the person receiving the value and the person paying for the solution should deliberately be different.
### 3. The Product Factory could create Micro-Brands rather than simply products
The Product Factory idea can be pushed another step.
Instead of ZedBiz becoming one company containing an expanding catalogue of unrelated products, the factory could create **small standalone brands built around one problem**.
Each micro-brand could have:
- One clearly defined problem
- One memorable name
- One flagship product
- One landing site
- One acquisition/distribution mechanism
- Its own customer or partner list
- Recurring or repeat economics where appropriate
- A documented production and operating system
- Minimal founder dependency
Illustrative structures — not current recommendations:
**MemberSpark** — tools community owners give members to create participation.
**AcreageReady** — rural-property decision tools distributed through realtors or lenders.
**ExitReady Digital** — business-sale digital-readiness tools distributed through brokers or accountants.
**CourseFinish** — implementation systems course creators add to courses to improve completion.
**LocalLaunchKit** — campaigns distributed by chambers, directories, agencies, or other local-business channels.
The names and markets are examples only. The important idea is the architecture.
ZedBiz becomes the **holding/manufacturing company** rather than necessarily the consumer-facing brand.
The internal engine becomes:
**Research → identify opportunity → create micro-brand → manufacture IP → establish distribution → systemize → grow, hold, license, sell, or kill.**
This connects directly to the Perfect Product requirement of building a sellable asset.
Instead of eventually trying to sell a complicated ZedBiz containing Jack, client work, experiments, and unrelated projects, a successful micro-brand could potentially be sold independently with its customer base, intellectual property, distribution relationships, revenue, and operating system intact.
The endgame therefore changes from:
> **Build one company with lots of products.**
to:
> **Build a factory capable of creating little companies.**
Some may die quickly. Some may become quiet small cash-flow assets. Occasionally one may become the major business.
## A new combined lens
Putting these three ideas together creates a different way to search for opportunities:
> **Find an end-user problem → identify the company financially hurt by that problem → manufacture a branded solution that company can deploy to its customers → repeat it across many channel owners → spin the successful solution into its own micro-brand.**
That is substantially different from asking:
> **What digital product should Jack sell?**
The more interesting discovery question may now be:
> **What recurring problems affect thousands of end users where somebody upstream has a stronger reason to pay for the solution than the end user does?**
That question deserves to remain open while the Perfect Product research continues.
