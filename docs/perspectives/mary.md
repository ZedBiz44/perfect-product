## Three new angles
### 1. You already bought the Perfect Product.
- The dot nobody connected: the Carex Hip Kit photo. Jack *is* the triggered buyer. He bought a labeled box at the exact moment of need — no webinar, no discovery call, no community, no onboarding sequence. Nobody nurtured him into it. The job landed and the box was sitting there.
- Stop designing for abstract "agency owners." Design for buyers who *are* Jack: blunt, competent, allergic to being sold to. They don't join Skools. They don't watch webinars. They buy the box at midnight.
- That market isn't small — it's every older operator, farmer, tradesperson, and small-town owner in Manus's niche directions. The brand writes itself: "No webinars. No discovery calls. No community. Just the box."
- The product spec becomes a person spec: *would Jack buy this at midnight?* If not, kill it.
### 2. One break, four products.
- The fleet breaks every other day — roughly 180 incidents a year. Right now each break is, at best, one field note.
- Run every incident through the factory *once* and get four products out: the story (the letter), the versioned artifact (the playbook tier), the standalone job kit (Manus's \$29–79 box), and the changelog entry (proof of maintenance).
- "Built once, sold many" applied to *production itself* — the factory is the productized asset, not any single product.
- The kicker nobody's selling: publish the hours. The whole thing visibly runs on about five hours a week. Radical transparency as the anti-guru proof — "the business you can verify runs without the founder." Nobody in this space can show that receipt. Jack can.
### 3. Build the shelf, not the box.
- Everyone — Manus, the GHL-kit page, Mary — has been designing individual kits. But [PLR.me](http://PLR.me) doesn't sell a pack. It sells *18,866* packs to 600,000 buyers. The shelf is the business.
- A tiny, ruthless shelf of one-job kits, labeled by job instead of by guru: "No-show recovery." "Review request engine." "Seasonal promotion." Self-serve, \$29–79 each, no discovery calls, no Jack.
- It starts with Jack's three kits (Manus's homework: three real jobs × six lines each), and every kit added makes the shelf more valuable.
- The shelf itself is the sellable asset — product line, customer list, transaction history — the exact thing a publisher would acquire, by construction.
- First placement: Jack's own directories. The flow he already owns, trait #9, no new audience to grow.
## Three dead concepts — kept as ingredients
*Jack killed all three on Sep 28, 2026. Kept here on his orders: a dead idea plus the right twist plus the perfect ingredient can become a good idea. Don't delete these.*
### 1. Client Retention Engine (dead)
- The idea: monthly white-labeled "Your Marketing This Month" narrative + one client-usable asset per agency client; \~\$5–10/client/mo; agency holds the GHL keys, no trust gate.
- Why Jack killed it: "Client retention is what GHL does. If we're selling to GHL owners and we're selling client retention engine, it's like — oh, you do client retention for your clients? How about we do that for you? ...we're dumb." It sold them their own job.
- Salvageable ingredient: the *question* it was trying to answer — "what am I paying for?" — is real. The error was selling retention-as-a-service instead of selling the agency a *weapon*.
### 2. Award-Submission Factory (dead)
- The idea: fleet turns agency GHL data into polished case studies + award packets, white-labeled, quarterly.
- Why Jack killed it: GHL awards run on GHL's own referral/affiliate data — there are no submissions. "Never heard of it. Can't see it being any value." Mary built on a casual mention instead of verifying. Sloppy.
- Salvageable ingredient: the *factory shape* — fleet turns the agency's raw data into polished proof assets. The error was the fake occasion (awards). The real occasion is the churn moment.
### 3. Wholesale Shelf (dead)
- The idea: 12 seasonal/local campaign kits a year, licensed flat \~\$197–497/mo, deployed across the agency's whole client base.
- Why Jack killed it: "Throw a whole bunch of shit at the wall and hope the GHL people buy it... that's like marketing 110." Generic commodity.
- Salvageable ingredient: the *distribution shape* — one agency sale reaches 50–1,000 end businesses, white-label, agency as distributor. The error was the contents (generic kits), not the channel.
**Standing rule from the kill:** no more framework-first products for a buyer Mary hasn't listened to first. Research owner words before inventing.
## What GHL agency owners actually say (listen-first research, Sep 28, 2026)
*The "HighLevel (GHL) Official Community" Facebook group was login-walled (no saved FB credentials), so fallback was public Reddit: \~9 full threads, 500+ comments across r/gohighlevel and r/agency. Software bug reports and feature requests excluded — business pains only, ranked by frequency.*
1. **Getting clients** (very common): "Cold outreach sucks. We all fucking hate it." The space's meme: buy GHL → fail to get clients → pivot to teaching GHL → students repeat the cycle.
2. **Fulfillment grind** (very common): "Troubleshooting execution logs at 10pm because a client's automation broke." "Broken automations, long nights fixing bugs while a client pings your phone at 2am."
3. **Pricing/packaging** (very common): "What are you actually doing to charge \$2-5k/mo instead of \$297?" Stuck at low SaaS tiers; underpricing because they don't track true per-client cost.
4. **Hiring** (very common): VAs who "couldn't complete basic tasks, even after test runs." Can't tell if a hire knows GHL "or just says they do." White-label fulfillment horror stories — one owner paid \$3,623 across four invoices for a build that arrived still broken after 7 months.
5. **Onboarding/support burden** (common): "I used to spend 2-3 hours on onboarding calls teaching clients the CRM... then they'd forget everything and Slack me to do it for them anyway."
6. **Churn** (common): "I ran traditional retainers for a year. Every month felt like I was re-convincing clients not to churn." And the key line: "Even when results are good, clients still ask 'what am I paying for?'"
7. **Reporting** (occasional, sharp): "Most agencies are doing solid work... but their reporting is messy. A mix of GHL reports, ad screenshots, and spreadsheets. It works, but it doesn't feel valuable to clients... I've seen agencies add a few hundred per client/month just by improving how they present data. GHL alone doesn't really solve this cleanly."
8. **Platform-risk churn** (the surprise): "Meta is actively banning Instagram accounts using native Comment-to-DM workflows. 3 of our accounts nuked this week" → "two of our users have already cancelled their packages... Our agency's revenue and reputation are taking a massive hit because a native GHL feature is treating user assets like sacrificial lambs." Agencies lose clients over platform enforcement they don't control.
9. **What they already pay for:** VAs/freelancers (\$150/mo overseas tech VAs), white-label fulfillment, snapshots, guru courses (\~\$997), prospecting/audit tools, CRM-simplifying Chrome extensions.
Also notable: SaaS-mode psychology — reframing retainer as "buying software, not hiring me" cut one owner's churn from \~25% to under 10%.
## Two new concepts from the research (Sep 28, 2026)
*Jack's brief: don't rebuild the three dead ones. Dig deep, off-the-wall, look broader. Two new concepts, built from the owners' words above. Status: ideas only.*
### 1. The Save Kit — a weapon for the churn moment, not a retention service
- The dot nobody connected: the dead Client Retention Engine + Manus's triggered-kit shape + Jack's own Hip Kit behavior (he buys at the moment of need — no webinar, no nurture). The churn quote is the trigger: "Even when results are good, clients still ask 'what am I paying for?'"
- The twist on the dead idea: don't sell the agency *retention-as-a-service* (their job — insulting). Sell them a *weapon they fire themselves* the moment a client wobbles: one click generates a white-labeled "here's everything we've done for you" proof packet — every automation run, every lead touched, every follow-up sent, every review gained, dollarized where possible — assembled from the agency's own GHL data, not a narrative we write.
- Triggered, not ambient: it fires when the question gets asked or cancellation looms. That's the Hip Kit shape — the labeled box sitting there at the moment of need.
- Why it clears the "don't sell them their job" rule: we're not doing their retention. We're arming it.
- The moat (NotionAI's performance-dataset idea, finally with a home): every save attempt tracked — fired, saved/lost, why. Over time that's a proprietary dataset of what actually stops churn, making every future kit smarter. The dataset is the sellable asset.
- Open validation: does GHL's API expose the workflow-execution and contact-touch data needed to auto-assemble the packet? If yes, buildable; if no, it dies here.
### 2. Platform Weather — early warning for the storms that cancel your clients
- The dot nobody connected: the Meta-nuking quote ("our agency's revenue and reputation are taking a massive hit") + the fleet's daily monitoring habit + the fact that here agencies don't lose clients to competitors — they lose them to *platform weather*.
- The product: monitoring + rapid response. The fleet watches Meta, Google, Twilio, and GHL itself for enforcement waves, policy changes, API deprecations, and breaking changes. When a storm hits, the agency gets (a) early warning before their clients feel it, and (b) pre-written white-labeled client comms — "here's what happened, it's platform-wide, here's our fix, here's what we're doing so it doesn't hit you again."
- The twist: it converts the agency's most humiliating moment (client's account banned, agency looks incompetent) into a heroism moment (agency warned them Tuesday, fix ready Wednesday). It sells *reputation armor*, not information.
- Between storms the monthly value is the brief itself — and GHL's own constant breakage (Jack's fleet breaks every other day on tool churn) means there's always weather.
- Broader than GHL: any business built on rented platforms. Starts with GHL agencies because that's where the quote came from.
- The sellable asset: the wave archive — every enforcement event documented with what broke, who got hit, and which comms worked. Nobody owns this dataset.
