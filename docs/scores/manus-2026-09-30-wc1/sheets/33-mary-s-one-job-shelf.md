## Assessment frame
- **Buyer and payer:** Working scenario: a capable local operator pays $49 for a self-serve review-request kit; the catalogue entry expressly leaves the final payer decision unresolved.
- **Offer and delivery:** A fixed, self-serve digital review-request kit: buyer selects the named job, enters business details, uses AI-generated request variants, tests the request, and places it in the customer journey. Custom installation and consulting are excluded.
- **Price hypothesis:** $49 per kit in the illustrative first-shelf scenario, within the recorded $29–$79 kit range; this is an unvalidated hypothesis competing with free review links, native tools, and instructions.
- **Repeat-payment mechanism:** Optional repeat purchases from additional locations and adjacent one-job kits; the initial review-request kit itself has no recurring charge or inherent replenishment cycle.
- **Founder role:** At practical steady state, a catalog operator maintains releases and bounded support while Jack selects occasional additions and exceptions, illustratively 0–2 hours per week. This depends on preserving fixed scope rather than turning installation or diagnosis into client work.
- **Primary satisfaction shape:** Tool in the hand: a capable operator completes a defined review-request setup and can immediately use the request mechanism.
- **Founder fit:** conditional

## Material assumptions
- Capable local operators have ordinary website/review-platform access and can complete the stated setup without custom assistance.
- A $49 fixed kit produces enough time, clarity, or review-generation benefit at a thin/ageing-review trigger to overcome free native links, free AI, and generic instructions.
- Structured inputs let AI produce usable request variants and instructions, while release review and exceptions remain bounded rather than becoming per-buyer human fulfillment.
- The business owns transferable rights to the kit IP, brand, checkout records, and permissioned customer/transaction relationships.
- Job-specific search or website-provider referrals can produce buyers at an acquisition cost and referral share compatible with a $49 sale, without Jack continuously selling.
- Adjacent fixed-scope kits and additional-location use create some repeat demand without the shelf accumulating unused inventory or drifting into bespoke work.

## Sensitivities
- The assessment changes materially if a $49 review-request kit cannot demonstrate a clear incremental benefit over free native review links, free AI prompts, or generic templates.
- Low ticket size makes the result sensitive to search/referral acquisition cost, referral incentive, refunds, and the real volume of customer support.
- Any requirement for bespoke website installation, workflow diagnosis, staff training, or review-policy troubleshooting weakens reuse, independence, C3PO, and ease simultaneously.
- Transferable-asset and scale assessments depend on retaining IP/data rights and on catalog additions being demand-led rather than a stockpile of unwanted kits.

## Improvements separate from score
- A declared narrow outcome, eligible operator profile, accepted input set, and fixed support boundary would make the self-serve promise and no-custom-work constraint testable.
- An explicit buyer-journey specification could distinguish ordinary placement steps from prerequisites that constitute a second implementation project.
- A stated acquisition-economics boundary for referral share, search cost, and support per purchase would clarify whether the $49 price can retain worthwhile margin.
- A documented ownership and transfer boundary for kit IP, checkout history, permissioned customer data, and platform dependencies would make the Gold claim more concrete.
- A demand-led admission rule for additional kits would preserve the shelf structure without treating inventory growth as evidence of value.

## Unresolved questions
- Which exact local-operator segment is the payer, and does it have both the review pain and the capability to self-implement?
- What differentiated result makes this kit worth $49 instead of a free link, native platform tool, or generic AI-generated request?
- Can website providers or search reach this payer repeatedly at economics compatible with a $49 one-time sale?
- What support, refund, platform-policy, and AI-output exceptions occur per buyer after an actual setup?
- Do additional locations and adjacent one-job kits create observed repeat demand, or does the shelf become a low-use library?
- Which rights and customer records are actually owned and transferable, given third-party review and website platforms?

## Overall interpretation
Under WC1, this is a coherent self-serve fixed-kit structure applied only to the illustrative $49 review-request kit, not a validation of a broad shelf or the rejected generic Wholesale Shelf. Reuse, bounded operations, Tool-in-the-hand satisfaction, and AI-supported delivery are credible under its explicit fixed-scope assumptions. Repeat demand, the paid-vs-free buyer case, and the acquisition route remain partial fits, while scale benefits are limited to shared costs and reusable operating assets rather than proven distribution gravity.
